**Part 1 — Postprandial glucose thresholds used in the major basal-plus / stepwise prandial trials**

**A. Guideline-level PPG targets (for context)**

SourcePPG targetTimingBasisReferencesADA Standards of Care 20261–2 h post-mealExpert consensus[1], [2]AACE/ACE 2023–20262 h post-mealExpert consensus[2], [3], [4]IDF1–2 h post-mealExpert consensus[2]

The ADA does not specify a single PPG threshold that should "trigger" prandial insulin; rather, it states that prandial insulin should be considered when basal insulin has been titrated to an acceptable fasting glucose, HbA1c remains above goal, and there is evidence of **significant postprandial hyperglycemia** or signs of overbasalization.[1] The AACE 2026 algorithm operationalizes overbasalization as **PPG >180 mg/dL (10 mmol/L) with FBG at goal, or basal dose >0.5 U/kg/day**.[4] These thresholds are expert consensus, not derived from a single primary trial.

**B. Trial-by-trial PPG thresholds**

**1. OPAL (Orals Plus Apidra and LANTUS) — Lankisch et al., 2008**

- PMID: not directly retrieved; published in Diabetes Obes Metab 2008; 10(12):1178–85.
- Design: Open-label RCT, 396 patients on glargine + OADs (HbA1c 6.5–9%, FBG ≤120 mg/dL), randomized to one injection of glulisine before breakfast vs. before the main meal, 24 weeks.
- **Trigger for prandial insulin initiation**: HbA1c 6.5–9% despite optimized basal insulin (FBG ≤120 mg/dL / 6.7 mmol/L). The trial enrolled patients already meeting this criterion; it did not use a specific PPG threshold to trigger initiation.
- **Titration target**: 2-h postprandial BG **≤135 mg/dL (≤7.5 mmol/L)**, at investigator discretion; FBG target ≤100 mg/dL (5.5 mmol/L).[5][6][7]
- **Timing**: 2 h after start of meal.
- **Primary endpoint (surrogate)**: Change in HbA1c. Result: HbA1c decreased by ~0.4% in both arms; 84.2% reached the 2-h PPG titration target on ≥1 visit; 30.7% reached HbA1c ≤6.5%.[6]
- Evidence type: **Open-label RCT** (no hard outcomes measured).

**2. Lankisch 1-2-3 study (Davidson et al., 2009)**

- Published in Endocr Pract 2009; 15(1):41–9 (PMID 19211396).
- Design: Open-label RCT, 343 patients on glargine + OADs with HbA1c >7%, randomized to 1, 2, or 3 daily injections of glulisine, 24 weeks.
- **Trigger**: HbA1c >7% despite optimized basal insulin.
- **Titration target**: The protocol used **pre-meal/bedtime BG targets** rather than postprandial targets. The retrieved literature describes HbA1c reductions of 0.44%, 0.36%, and 0.43% with 1, 2, or 3 injections, respectively. A specific 2-h PPG titration threshold traceable to the primary publication was not retrieved from the database; the trial used pre-next-meal glucose to guide dose adjustments.[8]
- Evidence type: **Open-label RCT** (surrogate endpoint only).

**3. FullSTEP (Rodbard et al., 2014)**

- PMID: **24622667**; Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2014; 2(1):30–7.[9]
- Design: Phase 4, open-label, non-inferiority RCT, 401 patients on basal insulin detemir + OADs (HbA1c 7–9%), randomized to stepwise addition of insulin aspart (1→2→3 boluses at weeks 0, 11, 22 if HbA1c ≥7%) vs. full basal-bolus from the start, 32 weeks.
- **Trigger for adding each successive bolus**: HbA1c ≥7% (53 mmol/mol) at weeks 11 and 22.[9]
- **Titration target**: Pre-meal SMPG **4.0–7.2 mmol/L (72–130 mg/dL)** — this was a **pre-meal** (not postprandial) target. The trial did not use a 2-h PPG threshold for dose titration.[9]
- **Primary endpoint (surrogate)**: Change in HbA1c at 32 weeks. Stepwise: −0.98%; basal-bolus: −1.12%; difference 0.14% (95% CI −0.02 to 0.30) — non-inferiority met. Hypoglycaemia rate ratio 0.58 (0.45–0.75) favoring stepwise.[9]
- Evidence type: **Open-label RCT** (surrogate endpoint only).

**4. 4-T (Treating to Target in Type 2 Diabetes) — Holman et al., 2007 & 2009**

- PMID: **17881754** (year 1, NEJM 2007); **19861578** (3-year, NEJM 2009).
- Design: Open-label RCT, 708 patients on metformin + sulfonylurea, randomized to biphasic insulin aspart BID, prandial insulin aspart TID, or basal insulin detemir QD, 3 years.[10][11]
- **Titration targets**: Pre-meal glucose **72–99 mg/dL (4.0–5.5 mmol/L)**; 2-h post-meal glucose **90–126 mg/dL (5.0–7.0 mmol/L)**.[11]
- **Timing**: 2 h after meals.
- **Trigger for adding second insulin (year 2–3)**: HbA1c >6.5% or unacceptable hyperglycemia (HbA1c >10% or two consecutive values ≥8% at/after 24 weeks).[11]
- **Results at 1 year (surrogate)**: HbA1c reduction: biphasic −1.3%, prandial −1.4%, basal −0.8%. Proportion ≤7%: biphasic 41.7%, prandial 48.7%, basal 27.8%. At 3 years: median HbA1c biphasic 7.1%, prandial 6.8%, basal 6.9% (p=0.28); proportion ≤6.5%: biphasic 31.9%, prandial 44.7%, basal 43.2%.[10][11]
- Weight gain highest in prandial group; hypoglycaemia highest in prandial group (5.7 events/patient/year vs. 1.7 basal).[11]
- Evidence type: **Open-label RCT** (surrogate endpoints only; no hard CV/mortality outcomes).

**5. AT.LANTUS — Davies et al., 2007 (sub-analysis); Fritsche et al., 2003**

- PMID sub-analysis: **17593236** (Diabetes Obes Metab 2007); original: PMID **14578243** (Diabetes Care 2003).
- Design: Open-label RCT, 4961 patients with T2DM, randomized to two glargine titration algorithms (investigator-driven vs. patient-driven), 24 weeks.[12]
- **Target**: FBG ≤**100 mg/dL (5.5 mmol/L)**. This trial addressed **basal insulin titration only** — it did not study prandial insulin addition or PPG targets. The "Fritsche" algorithm targeted FBG 90–110 mg/dL (5.0–6.1 mmol/L).[12][13]
- Evidence type: **Open-label RCT** (surrogate endpoint; basal titration only, no PPG-guided strategy).

**6. POC study (Owens/Riddle et al., 2011) — the "proof-of-concept" basal-plus study**

- PMID: **21824176**; Diabetes Obes Metab 2011; 13(11):1020–7.[14]
- Design: Open-label RCT, 106 patients randomized to basal-only vs. basal-plus-one-bolus (glulisine before main meal), 3 months after glargine optimization.
- **Titration target**: In UK/Russia: 2-h PPG **≤135 mg/dL (≤7.5 mmol/L)**; in USA: pre-meal/bedtime BG **100–120 mg/dL**.[5][14]
- Evidence type: **Open-label RCT** (surrogate endpoint only).

**Part 3 — Other trials testing whether targeting PPG improves hard outcomes**

**STOP-NIDDM (Chiasson et al., 2003)**

- PMID: **12876093**; JAMA 2003; 290(4):486–94.[17]
- Population: 1,368 patients with **IGT** (not T2DM on insulin), randomized to acarbose 100 mg TID vs. placebo, mean follow-up 3.3 years.
- **Primary endpoint**: Diabetes incidence (not CV). CV events were a **secondary endpoint**.
- CV result: HR 0.51 (95% CI 0.28–0.95, p=0.03) — 49% relative risk reduction. Absolute event rates: acarbose 15/714 (2.1%) vs. placebo 32/715 (4.5%); **absolute risk reduction ~2.4%, NNT ~42 over 3.3 years**.[17]
- **Critical limitations**: Secondary endpoint, very few events (n=47 total), high differential dropout (24% overall, more in acarbose arm), possible confounding by differential follow-up. This is widely regarded as **hypothesis-generating only**.[18]

**ACE (Acarbose Cardiovascular Evaluation) — Holman et al., 2017**

- PMID: **28711407**; Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2017; 5(11):877–86.[19]
- Population: 6,522 Chinese patients with **CHD + IGT**, randomized to acarbose 50 mg TID vs. placebo, median follow-up 5.0 years.
- **Primary endpoint (hard)**: 5-point MACE (CV death, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, hospitalization for unstable angina, hospitalization for HF).
- **Result**: Acarbose 470/3272 (14.4%, 3.33/100 person-years) vs. placebo 479/3250 (14.7%, 3.41/100 person-years); **HR 0.98 (95% CI 0.86–1.11, p=0.73)** — **no benefit**.[19]
- No difference in 3-point MACE, all-cause death, CV death, MI, stroke, or HF individually.[19]
- Acarbose reduced diabetes incidence by 18% (surrogate).[19]

**NAVIGATOR (Holman et al., 2010)**

- PMID: **20228404**; NEJM 2010; 362(16):1463–76.[20]
- Population: 9,306 patients with **IGT + CVD or CV risk factors**, randomized to nateglinide 60 mg TID vs. placebo (2×2 factorial with valsartan), median follow-up ~5 years.
- **Coprimary endpoints (hard)**: Core composite (CV death, nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, hospitalization for HF) and extended composite (core + unstable angina hospitalization + revascularization).
- **Results**:
  - Core composite: nateglinide 365/4645 (7.9%) vs. placebo 387/4661 (8.3%); **HR 0.94 (95% CI 0.82–1.09, p=0.43)**.[20]
  - Extended composite: nateglinide 658/4645 (14.2%) vs. placebo 707/4661 (15.2%); **HR 0.93 (95% CI 0.83–1.03, p=0.16)**.[20]
  - Diabetes incidence: **not reduced** (HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.00–1.15).[21]
- **No CV benefit from targeting postprandial glucose with nateglinide**.[20]

**Bottom line on hard outcomes**: There is **no RCT evidence that specifically targeting postprandial glucose (as opposed to fasting glucose or overall HbA1c) improves cardiovascular events, mortality, or microvascular endpoints** in T2DM. The three adequately powered trials that tested this hypothesis with hard endpoints — HEART2D, ACE, and NAVIGATOR — were all **negative**.[15][20][19] STOP-NIDDM suggested benefit, but this was a secondary endpoint with very few events and methodological concerns, graded as very low certainty.[17][18] The HEART2D post-hoc subgroup in patients >65.7 years (HR 0.69, 0.49–0.96) is hypothesis-generating only.[16]

All benefits demonstrated in the basal-plus/stepwise trials (FullSTEP, OPAL, 4-T, POC) are limited to **surrogate endpoints** (HbA1c, PPG excursions, glycaemic variability). No trial of prandial insulin intensification has been powered for, or demonstrated benefit on, hard clinical outcomes.

**Key finding**: Down-titration rules are **heterogeneous and often incompletely reported**. The INSIGHT and LANMET primary publications do not specify a fixed dose-reduction step. **Question 2: Stopping rules / ceiling dose**

The **0.5 U/kg/day** threshold is cited by both the AACE 2023 and 2026 algorithms as a marker of potential overbasalization, at which point prandial insulin or other intensification should be considered.[18][17] A second AACE criterion is a **bedtime-minus-morning glucose differential >50 mg/dL**.[17]

**Evidence base for the 0.5 U/kg/day threshold**: A post-hoc dose-response analysis by Umpierrez et al. (2019) of 12 RCTs of glargine U-100 (n=3,014) demonstrated that the **HbA1c-lowering effect per 0.1 U/kg dose increment diminishes markedly above 0.5 U/kg/day**, with a clear plateau in glycemic benefit.[19] This provides pharmacologic rationale but is a **post-hoc pooled analysis**, not a prospective trial endpoint.

Effect of 0.1‐IU/kg/d increases in insulin dose on the change in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c)

However, **no RCT has prospectively randomized patients to "stop at 0.5 U/kg" vs. "continue titrating"** to test whether this ceiling improves outcomes. The threshold is therefore best characterized as **expert consensus supported by pharmacokinetic/dose-response data** (GRADE: ⊕◯◯◯ Very low for the specific cutoff). Practical guidance suggests that basal insulin doses can reach **0.5–1.0 U/kg/day** in some patients, particularly those with significant insulin resistance, but that continued escalation without FPG improvement constitutes overbasalization and should prompt reassessment.[20][21]

---

**Question 3: The older / frail patient — slower titration, higher targets**

**Trial evidence**: The **SENIOR trial** (Ritzel 2018) is the only RCT specifically designed for patients ≥65 years. It used a **higher FBG target lower bound of 90 mg/dL** (vs. 80 mg/dL in most other trials) and a **lower down-titration trigger of [15] However, the SENIOR trial did not test a slower titration interval or a different step size for elderly patients — it used the same weekly titration schedule as other EDITION trials.

**No RCT has prospectively tested a slower titration step, a longer interval, or a higher fasting target specifically in patients ≥75 years or with frailty.** All recommendations in this area are **expert consensus only**:

- The **ADA 2026 Standards of Care** recommend less stringent HbA1c goals (180 mg/dL to prevent dehydration, infection, and hyperglycemic crises.[22]
- The **Endocrine Society 2019 guideline** recommends HbA1c targets of 7.5–8.5% depending on health status in patients ≥65 years, with the overarching recommendation to **minimize hypoglycemia** (GRADE 1|⊕⊕⊕◯). It does not specify a different titration step or interval.[23]
- The **FDA glargine label** states that in geriatric patients, "initial dosing, dosage increments, and maintenance dosage should be conservative to avoid hypoglycemic reactions."[24]
- A 2025 ADA position statement (Bolli et al.) recommends that for patients at higher risk of hypoglycemia, **conservative algorithms should be preferred** with "no more than 2 units of insulin dose change per week" and a **target FPG of 100–120 mg/dL (5.5–6.6 mmol/L)** rather than the tighter 80–100 mg/dL used in many trials.[21]

**GRADE appraisal for elderly/frail titration recommendations**: ⊕◯◯◯ Very low — based entirely on expert consensus, extrapolation from the SENIOR subgroup, and general principles of hypoglycemia avoidance. No trial has tested a specific titration algorithm in frail patients.

**PART 3 — HEAD-TO-HEAD COMPARISONS OF TITRATION ALGORITHMS (Network Meta-Analysis)**

A 2025 systematic review and network meta-analysis (Boonpattharatthiti et al., Diabetes Care 2025) compared titration strategies across multiple RCTs and found that **self-titration at least twice weekly with healthcare provider support** achieved the greatest HbA1c reduction compared with all other strategies (e.g., vs. conventional physician-led: mean difference −1.19%, 95% CI −1.67 to −0.72), **without increasing severe hypoglycemia risk**.[25]

**PART A — NON-INSULIN-TREATED TYPE 2 DIABETES**

The evidence consistently shows that routine SMBG in non-insulin-treated T2DM produces **statistically significant but clinically marginal HbA1c reductions** (~0.15–0.30%), with the effect driven almost entirely by **structured** SMBG programs that link results to treatment changes. No trial has demonstrated hard outcomes (CV events, mortality, microvascular endpoints). Below are the key trials.

**6. Farmer IPD Meta-Analysis (2012)**

- PMID: **22371867**; BMJ 2012; 344:e486.[8]
- Design: Individual patient data meta-analysis of **6 RCTs, 2,552 patients** with non-insulin-treated T2DM.

**7. Malanda Cochrane Review (2012)**

- Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2012; 1:CD005060.[9]
- Design: Systematic review of **12 RCTs** in non-insulin-treated T2DM.
- **Key conclusions**: SMBG in **newly diagnosed** T2DM was beneficial in lowering HbA1c. **8. Zou et al. Meta-Analysis (2023) — Optimal frequency**

- PMID: **36403159**; J Gen Intern Med 2023; 38(2):416–27.[10]
- Design: Systematic review and meta-analysis of **22 RCTs, 6,204 participants**.
- **Key frequency finding**: SMBG at **8–11 times/week** produced the greatest HbA1c reduction: **MD −0.35% (95% CI −0.51 to −0.20)**. Testing ≤7 times/week or >11 times/week showed smaller or non-significant effects.[10]

**PART B — INSULIN-TREATED TYPE 2 DIABETES**

**1. Nauck et al. (2014) — The only RCT of SMBG frequency in conventional insulin-treated T2DM**

- PMID: **24445534**; Diabetologia 2014; 57(5):886–96.[17]
- Design: Open-label, 2×2 factorial RCT, **300 patients** with T2DM on conventional insulin (basal or premixed ± OADs), 12 months.
- **Arms**: SMBG+ (once-weekly **4-point profiles**: fasting, pre-lunch, pre-dinner, bedtime = 4 tests/week) vs. SMBG− (no SMBG); crossed with HbA1c disclosure vs. non-disclosure.
- **Primary endpoint (surrogate)**: HbA1c at 12 months. SMBG+ reduced HbA1c by 0.3% from baseline; SMBG− also reduced by 0.3%. **Between-group difference: 0.0% (95% CI −0.2 to 0.2, p=0.93)** — no difference.[17]
- **Key finding**: Once-weekly 4-point profiles did **not** improve glycemic control in patients on conventional insulin, although indicators of hyperglycemia did increase the likelihood of therapy intensification.[17]
- **Hard outcomes**: None measured.
- GRADE: ⊕⊕◯◯ Low (open-label, moderate size, surrogate endpoint; the testing frequency may have been too low to detect an effect).

**2. Structured profiles in insulin titration trials**

No RCT has directly compared different SMBG frequencies (e.g., daily vs. every-3-days vs. weekly fasting glucose) for basal insulin titration. The evidence for SMBG in insulin-treated T2DM comes **indirectly** from the titration trials discussed in the previous response (Treat-to-Target, AT.LANTUS, PREDICTIVE 303, etc.), all of which required **daily fasting SMBG** as the basis for dose adjustment. The 2025 network meta-analysis by Boonpattharatthiti et al. (Diabetes Care 2025) found that self-titration **at least twice weekly** with healthcare provider support achieved the greatest HbA1c reduction, implying a minimum of **2–3 fasting glucose measurements per titration interval** (i.e., at least daily or every-other-day testing during active titration).[18]

**3. ADA/AACE guideline recommendations for insulin-treated patients**

- **Basal insulin only**: The ADA 2026 Standards and the JAMA review (Kalyani et al., 2025) state that **once-daily fasting BGM can suffice** to achieve fasting glucose targets and reduce HbA1c.[19][20]
- **Multiple daily injections (MDI)**: BGM should be performed **at a minimum prior to meals and at bedtime** (i.e., ≥4 tests/day) to guide insulin dosing and avoid hypoglycemia.[19]
- **CGM**: Should be offered to all people with T2DM taking insulin.[19]

---

**PART C — MINIMUM SMBG DENSITY FOR SAFE INSULIN TITRATION**

**There is no RCT that has directly tested a "minimum SMBG density" below which basal insulin titration becomes unsafe.** The answer is therefore **pragmatic, not evidence-based**:

- All major basal insulin titration algorithms (Treat-to-Target, AT.LANTUS, PREDICTIVE 303, INSIGHT, LANMET) required **daily fasting SMBG** as the data input for dose adjustment. The titration intervals ranged from every 3 days (INSIGHT, AT.LANTUS patient arm) to weekly (Treat-to-Target), meaning **3–7 fasting values per titration cycle** were used to calculate the mean FBG that drove dose changes.[18]
- The Boonpattharatthiti NMA (2025) provides the strongest indirect evidence: titration **at least twice weekly** (requiring at minimum 2–3 FBG values between adjustments) was superior to less frequent titration. This implies that **fewer than ~3 fasting glucose measurements per week during active titration** is likely insufficient to safely guide dose changes, though this threshold has never been prospectively validated.[18]
- The ADA 2026 Technology Standards explicitly state: "The evidence is insufficient regarding when to prescribe BGM and how often monitoring is needed for insulin-treated people with diabetes who do not use intensive insulin therapy."[20]

**GRADE for the "minimum density" question**: ⊕◯◯◯ Very low — no direct evidence; the threshold is entirely pragmatic, derived from the testing protocols embedded in titration trial designs.

**PART A — ATTRIBUTION LOGIC: MAPPING TIMING OF HYPOGLYCEMIA TO THE RESPONSIBLE INSULIN COMPONENT**

The classic "pre-meal attribution" logic — which insulin component to adjust based on when hypoglycemia occurs — is codified most explicitly in the **AACE 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline** (Blonde et al., Endocr Pract 2022). This guideline provides a table titled "Recommended Steps for the Intensification of Insulin Therapy When Prandial Control Is Needed" that maps hypoglycemia timing to dose adjustment as follows:[1]

Timing of low BGInsulin component to reduceDose changeReferencesLow **fasting** BGReduce **bedtime (HS) basal** dose−10% to −20%[1]Low **pre-midday meal** BGReduce **breakfast prandial** dose−10% to −20%[1]Low **pre-evening meal** BGReduce **midday prandial** dose−10% to −20%[1]Low **bedtime** BGReduce **evening prandial** dose−10% to −20%[1]

[1]

The same logic is applied in reverse for hyperglycemia (up-titration): elevated fasting BG → increase HS basal; elevated pre-midday BG → increase breakfast prandial; elevated pre-evening BG → increase midday prandial; elevated bedtime BG → increase dinner prandial.[1]

The **AACE 2023 algorithm** (Samson et al.) provides a similar prandial titration scheme:[3]

- Pre-midday meal glucose >110 mg/dL → increase morning meal dose by 10–20%
- Pre-evening glucose >140 mg/dL → increase midday meal dose by 10–20%
- Bedtime glucose >140 mg/dL → increase pre-evening meal dose by 10%

The **JAMA 2025 review** (Kalyani et al.) states: "Basal insulin can be used to target elevated fasting glucose levels (>80–130 mg/dL). Prandial insulin can be used to target elevated postprandial glucose levels within 1–2 h after meal (>180 mg/dL)." The ADA 2026 Standards note that "when significant additions to the prandial insulin dose are made, particularly with the evening meal, consideration should be given to decreasing basal insulin to reduce risk of hypoglycemia."[4][2]

**Critical appraisal: This attribution logic rests entirely on physiological reasoning, not on empirical data.** The principle is that each insulin component's pharmacodynamic peak corresponds to a specific time window, and therefore hypoglycemia occurring in that window is attributed to that component. **No RCT has tested whether adjusting insulin according to this time-based attribution logic produces better outcomes (fewer hypoglycemic events, better HbA1c) than any alternative approach.** The logic is plausible — rapid-acting insulin peaks 1–3 hours post-injection, so pre-next-meal hypoglycemia is likely attributable to the preceding prandial dose — but this has never been validated prospectively. GRADE: ⊕◯◯◯ Very low (expert consensus based on pharmacokinetic reasoning).

**The four-period day division**: The AACE table above effectively uses a **four-period day** (fasting/overnight → morning-to-midday → midday-to-evening → evening-to-bedtime), anchored to meals rather than clock time. **No guideline or trial retrieved uses a fixed clock-time four-period division** (e.g., 00:00–06:00, 06:00–12:00, 12:00–18:00, 18:00–24:00) for insulin dose attribution. The meal-anchored approach is universal in clinical practice, which introduces variability depending on individual meal timing. The in-hospital DIATEC trial (Olsen et al., 2025) used a two-period division — nocturnal (fasting) glucose to guide basal insulin and daytime (nonfasting) glucose to guide prandial insulin — but not a four-period scheme.[5]

**6. Bolli et al. (2019) — 24-hour distribution and nocturnal window analysis**

- PMID: **30338618**; Diabetes Obes Metab 2019; 21(2):378–87.[12]
- Design: Post-hoc pooled analysis of EDITION 2, 3, and JP 2 trials (N=1,922, basal-only insulin), comparing Gla-300 vs. Gla-100.
- **Key finding**: Hypoglycemic events were **most frequent between 06:00 and 08:00 AM** — immediately after the standard nocturnal window ends. **CGM improves glycemic control compared with SMBG in basal-insulin-treated T2DM**, with consistent HbA1c reductions of **−0.4% to −0.6%** and TIR improvements of **+15 percentage points** across the key RCTs, while simultaneously **reducing hypoglycemia**. However, all evidence to date remains on **surrogate endpoints** — no RCT has demonstrated hard outcomes (CV events, mortality). Below is the trial-by-trial evidence.

**PART 1 — KEY RCTs: CGM vs. SMBG IN BASAL-INSULIN-TREATED T2DM**

**1. MOBILE (Martens et al., JAMA 2021)**

- PMID: **34077499**; JAMA 2021; 325(22):2262–72.[1]
- Design: Open-label, multicenter RCT, **175 adults** with T2DM on basal insulin without prandial insulin, managed in **primary care**, 8 months. Randomized 2:1 to real-time CGM (Dexcom G6) vs. BGM.
- Population: Mean age 57 years; 50% female; 53% racial/ethnic minority; mean baseline HbA1c **9.1%**; only **27%** on SGLT2i or GLP-1 RA.[1][2]
- **Primary endpoint (surrogate)**: HbA1c at 8 months: CGM **8.0%** vs. BGM **8.4%**; adjusted difference **−0.4% (95% CI −0.8 to −0.1; p=0.02)**.[1]
- **Key secondary endpoints (surrogates)**: TIR 70–180 mg/dL: CGM **59%** vs. BGM **43%** (adjusted difference **+15%, 95% CI 8 to 23; p250 mg/dL: CGM **11%** vs. BGM **27%** (adjusted difference **−16%; p[1]
- **Hypoglycemia (surrogate)**: Time [1]
- **Mechanism**: The MOBILE study did not mandate a specific CGM-based titration algorithm. Improvements were driven by patient behavioral changes informed by real-time glucose data and clinician treatment adjustments.[1]
- **Subgroup analysis** (Davis et al., 2022; PMID 34962151): The greatest CGM benefit was in those with worst baseline control — participants with HbA1c ≥10% had a **32 percentage-point greater TIR increase** with CGM vs. BGM.[3]

**2. FreeDM2 (Wilmot et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2026)**

- PMID: **42035781**; Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2026; 14(6):463–74.[2]
- Design: Open-label, multicenter RCT, **303 adults** with T2DM on basal insulin + SGLT2i and/or GLP-1 RA/dual GIP/GLP-1 RA (95% on these agents), 24 UK primary and secondary care centers, 32 weeks. CGM users reported being more active and making healthier dietary choices.[2]
- **Hypoglycemia**: Two severe hypoglycemic events occurred — both in the **SMBG control group**. No severe hypoglycemia in the CGM group.[2]
- **Sensor adherence**: Median sensor usage **97.6%** (phase 1) and **98.2%** (phase 2).[2]
- **Hard outcomes**: None measured (32-week duration).

**3. Steno2tech (Lind et al., Diabetes Care 2024)**

- PMID: **38489032**; Diabetes Care 2024; 47(5):869–78.[4]
- Design: Single-center, open-label RCT, **76 adults** with inadequately controlled insulin-treated T2DM (median baseline HbA1c 8.3%), 12 months. CGM (Dexcom G6) vs. BGM.
- **Primary endpoint (surrogate)**: TIR 3.9–10.0 mmol/L: between-group difference **+15.2% (95% CI 4.6 to 25.9)** favoring CGM.[4]
- **Additional surrogates**: HbA1c: between-group difference **−0.9% (95% CI −1.4 to −0.3)**. Total daily insulin dose: **−10.6 U/day (95% CI −19.9 to −1.3)**. Weight: **−3.3 kg (95% CI −5.5 to −1.1)**. BMI: **−1.1 kg/m² (95% CI −1.8 to −0.3)**.[4]
- **Notable**: This is the only RCT showing simultaneous improvements in glycemia, insulin dose reduction, **and weight loss** with CGM in T2DM.
- **Hard outcomes**: None measured.

**4. Kim et al. (Diabetologia 2024) — isCGM + structured education in MDI-treated T2DM**

- PMID: **38639876**; Diabetologia 2024; 67(7):1223–34.[5]
- Design: Open-label, multicenter RCT, **159 adults** with T2DM on MDI (HbA1c 7.5–12%), 24 weeks. Three arms: isCGM + structured education on pattern interpretation vs. isCGM + conventional education vs. BGM + conventional education.
- **Primary endpoint (surrogate)**: HbA1c change at 24 weeks: isCGM + structured education **−1.00%** vs. isCGM alone **−0.63%** (p=0.037) vs. BGM **−0.58%** (p=0.019 vs. structured education group).[5]

**PART 2 — CGM-BASED INSULIN TITRATION ALGORITHMS**

**5. El Fathi et al. (Diabetes Technol Ther 2026) — Algorithmic CGM-based titration**

- PMID: **41651803**; Diabetes Technol Ther 2026.[6]
- Design: Two-site, open-label RCT, **30 adults** with T2DM (HbA1c 7–9%) on degludec ± noninsulin agents, without rapid-acting insulin, 16 weeks. Randomized 2:1 to weekly algorithmic CGM-based dose changes with open CGM (EXP) vs. weekly SMBG-based titration with blinded CGM (CTR).
- **CGM titration algorithm**: Weekly dose adjustments based on CGM-derived metrics (specific algorithm details in protocol). Both groups received dose notifications via phone.
- **Primary endpoint (surrogate)**: TIR change: EXP **+20.3 percentage points** (54.1% → 75.3%) vs. CTR **+8.3 percentage points** (50.2% → 55.3%). Estimated treatment difference: **+14.6 percentage points (one-sided 95% CI lower bound +4.0; p[6]
- **Hypoglycemia**: Time [6]
- **Hard outcomes**: None measured.
- GRADE: ⊕⊕◯◯ Low (very small, open-label, feasibility trial, surrogate endpoints).

**6. Martens et al. (2025) — CGM "1-h AM nadir" as FBG surrogate for titration**

- PMID: **40683222**; Diabetes Metab Syndr 2025.[7]
- Design: Retrospective analysis of 7,354 paired FBG/blinded CGM data points from 68 patients with T2DM.
- **Key finding**: The **median of the 3 lowest CGM values in the hour preceding the FBG timepoint** ("1-h AM nadir") was essentially equivalent to FBG for basal insulin titration across three standard algorithms (INSIGHT, Treat-to-Target, AT.LANTUS). Relative dose errors were mostly within ±10%, indicating high agreement and low clinical risk.[7]
- **Implication**: CGM can replace fasting fingerstick for basal insulin titration using existing algorithms, without requiring new titration rules.

**PART 3 — META-ANALYTIC EVIDENCE**

**7. Lian et al. (Front Endocrinol 2025) — Systematic review and meta-analysis**

- PMID: **41659334**; Front Endocrinol 2025; 16:1530780.[8]
- 11 RCTs, 789 participants (CGM n=437, SMBG n=352).
- **Results**: CGM vs. SMBG: HbA1c **MD −0.20%**; TIR **MD +7.41%**; time above range **MD −6.93%**; time below range **MD −0.26%**; glucose variability (CV) **MD −1.06%**. No significant differences in cardiometabolic or psychosocial outcomes.[8]

**8. Bergenstal et al. (JAMA 2025) — CGM review**

- JAMA 2025; 334(24):2175–86.[9]
- Summarizes: In the basal-insulin-only population (MOBILE), HbA1c improved from 9.1% to 8.0% with CGM vs. 9.0% to 8.4% with BGM (p=0.02). A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs (1,248 participants) across the spectrum of T2DM regimens reported a mean HbA1c difference of **−0.31% (95% CI −0.43 to −0.19)** favoring CGM.[9]

**PART 4 — DOES CGM IMPROVE HARD OUTCOMES?**

**No RCT has demonstrated that CGM improves hard outcomes (CV events, mortality, microvascular endpoints) in T2DM.** However, two large observational studies provide suggestive evidence:

**9. Reaven et al. (Diabetes Technol Ther 2025) — VA cohort**

- PMID: **40432529**; Diabetes Technol Ther 2025; 27(10):715–24.[10]
- Design: Propensity-score-matched retrospective cohort, **12,729 CGM initiators** matched 1:1 with non-CGM users, VA Health Care System, 18-month follow-up.
- Population: 94% male, mean age 66, insulin-treated T2DM.
- **Results (hard outcomes)**:
  - **All-cause mortality**: HR **0.79 (95% CI 0.73–0.86)** — 21% relative reduction.
  - **All-cause hospitalization**: HR **0.91 (95% CI 0.86–0.96)**.
  - **Cardiovascular events**: HR **0.84 (95% CI 0.73–0.96)**.
  - **Hyperglycemia-related admissions**: HR **0.88 (95% CI 0.81–0.95)**.
  - Results persisted after accounting for early deaths, COVID-19, subsequent use of insulin pumps or newer medications, and when stratified by frailty index or mortality risk.[10]
- **Limitations**: Observational; unmeasured confounders (healthy-user bias) cannot be excluded.
- GRADE: ⊕⊕◯◯ Low (observational, propensity-matched but residual confounding likely).

**10. Karter et al. (JAMA 2021) — Kaiser Permanente cohort**

- PMID: **34077502**; JAMA 2021; 325(22):2273–84.[11]
- Design: Retrospective difference-in-differences analysis, 41,753 insulin-treated patients (5,673 T1DM + 36,080 T2DM), 12-month pre/post comparison.
- **Results**: CGM initiation associated with HbA1c reduction **−0.40% (95% CI −0.48 to −0.32; p[11]
- GRADE: ⊕⊕◯◯ Low (observational, selection bias likely).

**PART 5 — TIR AS A SURROGATE FOR HARD OUTCOMES**

The clinical relevance of CGM-derived TIR improvements is supported by observational data linking TIR to hard outcomes:

**11. Lu et al. (Diabetes Care 2021)**

- PMID: **33097560**; Diabetes Care 2021; 44(2):549–55.[12]
- Design: Prospective cohort, **6,225 adults** with T2DM, baseline CGM, median follow-up **6.9 years**.
- **Results**: Compared with TIR >85%, TIR ≤50% was associated with: all-cause mortality HR **1.83 (95% CI 1.48–2.28)**; CV mortality HR **1.85 (95% CI 1.25–2.72)**. Dose-response relationship: p for trend [12]

**12. Qu et al. (J Clin Med 2026) — Meta-analysis**

- PMID: **42513627**; J Clin Med 2026.[13]
- 24 observational studies, 35,916 participants. Each **10% increment in TIR** was associated with: all-cause mortality OR **0.88 (95% CI 0.82–0.93)**; diabetic retinopathy OR **0.92 (95% CI 0.89–0.95)**; peripheral neuropathy OR **0.77 (95% CI 0.71–0.84)**; lower extremity atherosclerotic disease OR **0.86 (95% CI 0.82–0.91)**.[13]

**BOTTOM LINE**

CGM consistently improves surrogate glycemic outcomes (HbA1c −0.4 to −0.9%, TIR +15 percentage points) compared with SMBG in basal-insulin-treated T2DM, while simultaneously reducing hypoglycemia. The FreeDM2 trial extends this to patients on modern combination therapy (basal insulin + SGLT2i/GLP-1 RA). CGM-derived metrics can replace fasting fingerstick for basal insulin titration with >90% dose-agreement. However, **all RCT evidence remains on surrogate endpoints**. The observational VA cohort (Reaven 2025) showing a mortality HR of 0.79 is hypothesis-generating but cannot establish causality. The ADA now recommends offering CGM to all insulin-treated T2DM patients, and the evidence supports this as a replacement for — not merely a supplement to — SMBG-based titration.[15][16]

**1 — RECOMMENDED SMBG FREQUENCY BY INSULIN REGIMEN**

**ADA Standards of Care 2026 (Section 7, "Diabetes Technology")**

The ADA **does not prescribe a specific number of daily tests** for patients on basal insulin only. The relevant recommendations are:

- **Rec 7.11 (Grade B)**: "People who are taking insulin and using BGM should be encouraged to check their blood glucose levels when appropriate based on their insulin therapy. This may include checking when fasting, prior to meals and snacks, after meals, at bedtime, in the middle of the night, prior to, during, and after exercise, when hypoglycemia is suspected, after treating low blood glucose levels until achieving normoglycemia, when hyperglycemia is suspected, and prior to and while performing critical tasks such as driving."[1]
- For **basal insulin ± OADs/noninsulin injectables**: "The evidence is insufficient regarding when to prescribe BGM and how often monitoring is needed for insulin-treated people with diabetes who do not use intensive insulin therapy." However, "for those taking basal insulin, assessing fasting glucose with BGM to inform dose adjustments to achieve blood glucose goals results in lower A1C levels."[1]
- For **intensive insulin therapy (MDI or pump)**: "Most individuals on intensive insulin therapies should be encouraged to assess glucose levels using BGM (and preferably CGM with BGM backup) prior to meals and snacks, at bedtime, occasionally postprandially, prior to, during, and after physical activity, when they suspect hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia, after treating hypoglycemia until they are normoglycemic, and prior to and while performing critical tasks such as driving. For many individuals using BGM alone, this requires doing finger sticks **6–10 times daily**, although individual needs may vary."[1]

The JAMA 2025 review (Kalyani et al.) synthesizes this as: "For patients only taking basal insulin, **once-daily fasting BGM** can suffice to achieve fasting glucose targets (80–130 mg/dL) and reduce HbA1C." For MDI: "BGM be performed **at a minimum prior to meals and at bedtime** to guide insulin dosing and avoid hypoglycemia."[2]

**AACE 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline (Blonde et al.)**

- **Rec 3.2 (Grade A, BEL 1)**: "All persons who use insulin should use CGM or perform BGM **a minimum of twice daily** and ideally before any insulin injection. More frequent BGM may be needed by persons who are taking multiple insulin injections, persons not at A1C targets, or those with history of hypoglycemia."[3]

**ADA/EASD 2022 Consensus Report (Davies et al., PMID 36151309)**

The retrieved text of the ADA/EASD 2022 consensus report does not contain a specific SMBG frequency recommendation for insulin-treated T2DM. The report focuses on CGM as the preferred monitoring modality and defers to the ADA Standards of Care for BGM specifics. **A specific SMBG testing schedule cannot be grounded in this document from the text retrieved.**[4]

**4 — BASAL INSULIN DOSE CEILING**

**No guideline defines a hard pharmacological ceiling** for basal insulin dose. However, two guidelines define **overbasalization** as a signal to stop up-titrating and intensify differently:

**ADA 2026 (Section 9)**

- "Clinicians should also be aware of the potential for **overbasalization** with insulin therapy (i.e., use of higher than clinically necessary and appropriate dose of basal insulin, typically masking insufficient mealtime insulin)."[20]
- **No numeric dose ceiling is stated.** The ADA does not specify a U/kg/day threshold. The concept is defined qualitatively, not quantitatively. **Grade: not graded** — this is a narrative statement, not a numbered recommendation.

**AACE 2023/2026**

The AACE provides the most explicit numeric threshold:

- "So-called overbasalization may be an issue if there are elevated PPG levels (>180 mg/dL or 10 mmol/L) but the FBG is at goal **or if the basal insulin dose is >0.5 units/kg/day**."[12][13]
- At that point, prandial insulin should be considered. The AACE 2023 adds: "If the basal insulin dose is >0.5 units/kg/day **or the bedtime minus morning prebreakfast glucose score is >50 mg/dL**, prandial insulin should be considered."[13]
- **Evidence basis**: This 0.5 U/kg/day threshold is **expert consensus**. It is not derived from a specific RCT that tested outcomes above vs. below this dose. It reflects the clinical observation that beyond ~0.5 U/kg/day, further basal insulin increases yield diminishing returns on fasting glucose while increasing hypoglycemia risk and weight gain, and that residual hyperglycemia is increasingly postprandial rather than fasting.

**Mehta et al. (2021, PMID 34165382) — Narrative review**

- "The dose of basal insulin should be increased as required up to approximately **0.5–1.0 units/kg/day** in some cases. Overbasalization (continuing to escalate dose without a meaningful reduction in fasting plasma glucose) is not recommended."[21]

**Bolli et al. (Diabetes Care 2025)**

- "The final BI dose may vary considerably from 0.2 to 0.3 units/kg to hundreds of units per day, requiring bigger steps and longer titration." This explicitly acknowledges that **no absolute ceiling exists** and that highly insulin-resistant patients may require doses well above 0.5 U/kg/day.[22]

2. **Capillary glucose targets**: The preprandial 80–130 mg/dL and postprandial [10][12]
3. **De-intensification**: No guideline provides a specific capillary glucose floor for insulin reduction in older adults. The ACP's HbA1c [16][15]
4. **Basal insulin dose ceiling**: The 0.5 U/kg/day threshold (AACE) is expert consensus, never validated in an RCT. The ADA deliberately avoids a numeric ceiling.[20][12]
