**2. French-Language Validation**

- **SCOFF — Yes, formally validated in French (SCOFF-F).** Two studies by Garcia et al. provide full linguistic and psychometric validation:
  - Garcia et al. (2010): Translation/back-translation, validated in **400 female university students** (mean age 21 y) in France against MINI/DSM-IV. Sensitivity **94.6%**, specificity **94.8%**, AUC 96.2%.[9]
  - Garcia et al. (2011): Validated in a **clinical nutrition unit** population (67 AN, 45 BN, 114 healthy controls) in France. Sensitivity **94.6%**, specificity **94.7%**, AUC 97.9%, Cohen's kappa 89%.[10]
- **EDS-PC — No published French validation** was identified in the literature.
- **DEPS-R — No published French validation** was identified. Validated translations exist in Italian, Dutch, Mandarin Chinese, and Norwegian, but not French.[11][12][13][14]
- **Binge Eating Scale (BES) and Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ)** — which the ADA recommends for T2D — also lack clearly documented French validation studies in the retrieved literature, though a Portuguese version of the BES exists.[15]

---

**3. Validation Specifically in Adults with Type 2 Diabetes**

This is a critical gap. **None of the three instruments (SCOFF, EDS-PC, DEPS-R) has been formally validated with diagnostic accuracy data specifically in an adult type 2 diabetes population.**

- The **DEPS-R** was developed and validated in type 1 diabetes. Its insulin-specific items (e.g., restricting rapid-acting insulin for weight control) are not applicable to many patients with T2D. To address this, the **DEPS-10** was recently developed by removing insulin-specific items and was tested in a mixed T1D/T2D sample (n = 679, including T2D patients), showing AUC 0.92 for detecting BED — but this is a new instrument with limited validation.[8][14][7]
- The Italian DEPS-R validation by Pinna et al. (2017) included **insulin-treated** patients with both T1D and T2D (n = 211), but did not report separate diagnostic accuracy for the T2D subgroup.[11]
- The **SCOFF** has been validated in general adult populations (primary care, university, psychiatric clinics) but never specifically in a diabetes cohort.[2][3]
- The **EDS-PC** was validated in university students and female veterans, not in diabetes populations.[2][4]

---

**4. Guideline Recommendations for Eating Disorder Screening in Type 2 Diabetes**

The **ADA Standards of Care 2026** provide the most specific guidance:

- **Recommendation 5.50** (Grade B): Screen for disordered or disrupted eating using **validated screening measures** and review the treatment plan for potential effects on hunger/caloric intake.[16]
- **Recommendation 5.51** (Grade B): Reevaluate the treatment plan in patients presenting with disordered eating symptoms, ideally in consultation with a qualified professional.[16]
- For **type 2 diabetes specifically**, the ADA recommends the **Questionnaire on Eating and Weight Patterns-5 (QEWP-5)**, the **Binge Eating Scale (BES)**, and the **Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ)** — not the DEPS-R, which is reserved for T1D.[16]
- The ADA explicitly states that in T2D, **binge eating and night eating syndrome** are the most commonly reported disordered eating patterns.[16]
- Importantly, the ADA also recommends that during weight management and medical nutrition therapy encounters, patients should be **screened for a history of dieting and past or current disordered eating behaviors** before initiating structured weight loss programs.[16][17]

The **USPSTF (2022)** issued a Grade I statement (insufficient evidence) regarding routine screening for eating disorders in the general adult population, noting that the SCOFF has adequate accuracy but no direct trial evidence links screening to improved outcomes.[1][2] The **APA (2023)** recommends screening as part of any initial psychiatric evaluation, including assessment of restrictive eating, binge eating, and compensatory behaviors, with diabetes mellitus listed as a relevant comorbidity.[18]

**Summary of Practical Implications**

For a French-speaking primary care practice managing adults with T2D:

- The **SCOFF-F** is the only instrument among the three that has a validated French version, but it was not validated in a diabetes population and performs best for AN/BN rather than binge eating disorder — the predominant eating disorder phenotype in T2D.
- The ADA-recommended instruments for T2D (QEWP-5, BES, NEQ) lack published French validations and have not been validated specifically in T2D with diagnostic interview reference standards.
- The **DEPS-10** is a promising new diabetes-specific tool applicable to T2D, but it is very new (2025) and has no French version.
- This represents a significant **unmet need**: no brief, validated, French-language, diabetes-specific eating disorder screening instrument exists for adults with type 2 diabetes.

**1. Nocebo Effect and Statin Rechallenge: Evidence from Blinded Trials**

The strongest evidence that **most statin-attributed muscle symptoms are not pharmacologically caused** comes from two landmark n-of-1/crossover trials:

- **SAMSON** (n = 60, UK): A triple-arm, 12-month crossover trial (statin vs. placebo vs. no tablet, each 4 months). Symptom intensity on a 0–100 VAS was **16.3 for statin, 15.4 for placebo, and 8.0 for no tablet**. The "nocebo ratio" — the proportion of statin-attributed symptoms also elicited by placebo — was **90%**. Early tablet cessation was 21.6% for statin vs. 17.2% for placebo. After unblinding, **50% of participants successfully reinitiated long-term statin therapy**.[1][2]
- **StatinWISE** (n = 200, UK primary care): Six double-blind 2-month periods of atorvastatin 20 mg vs. placebo. Mean muscle symptom scores showed **no significant difference** (statin minus placebo: −0.11; 95% CI −0.36 to 0.14; P = 0.40). Withdrawals for intolerable symptoms were 9% during statin vs. 7% during placebo periods. **Two-thirds of completers restarted long-term statin therapy**.[3][4]

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of 8 blinded rechallenge RCTs (n = 1,817) found that on blinded rechallenge, **only one-third (36%) of previously "statin-intolerant" patients were unable to tolerate the statin**, while one-quarter (26%) were also intolerant of placebo (NNH = 10). Critically, there was **no significant difference in mean myalgia scores** between statin and placebo (MD 1.08 on a 100-point scale; 95% CI −1.51 to 3.67).[5] Retrospective data suggest that with unblinded rechallenge (different statin or dose), **>90% of patients can ultimately tolerate a statin** at 1 year.[6][7]

---

**2. Intermittent / Non-Daily and Lower-Dose Statin Strategies**

**Surrogate endpoint (LDL-C):** A meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (n = 1,023) found **no significant difference in LDL-C reduction** between alternate-day and daily dosing of atorvastatin (MD 6.8 mg/dL; P = 0.11) or rosuvastatin (MD 10.5 mg/dL; P = 0.06). Daily dosing was modestly superior for total cholesterol but not for LDL-C or triglycerides. This is pharmacologically plausible given the long half-lives of atorvastatin (~14 h active metabolites) and rosuvastatin (~19 h).[8][9]

**Hard cardiovascular outcomes:** No RCT has evaluated intermittent statin dosing on MACE. The ACC/AHA 2026 guideline and the JACC expert consensus acknowledge this gap explicitly: "the efficacy of intermittent statin dosing on ASCVD outcomes has not been established." A retrospective cohort (n = 1,605) showed a non-significant trend toward decreased mortality with intermittent dosing vs. no statin, but this is low-quality evidence.[10][11][6]

**Bottom line:** Alternate-day atorvastatin or rosuvastatin achieves **~70–80% of the LDL-C reduction** of daily dosing and is a reasonable strategy for partial intolerance, but there is **no hard outcomes evidence** to support it.

**4. Specific Considerations in Type 2 Diabetes**

Patients with T2D derive **equal or greater absolute benefit** from LDL-C lowering compared to those without diabetes, because of their higher baseline ASCVD risk:

- **Ezetimibe (IMPROVE-IT):** The diabetes subgroup (27% of trial, n = 4,933) had a **5.5% ARR** in the primary composite (NNT ~18 over 7 years), compared with a non-significant 0.7% ARR in those without diabetes (P for interaction = 0.02). Reductions in MI (24% RRR) and ischemic stroke (39% RRR) were particularly pronounced.[13][12]
- **Bempedoic acid (CLEAR Outcomes):** In the prespecified diabetes subanalysis (n = 6,373, 46% of trial), bempedoic acid reduced MACE-4 with HR 0.83 (95% CI 0.72–0.95; **ARR 2.4%**), with no evidence of effect modification across glycemic strata (interaction P = 0.42). Crucially, bempedoic acid did **not increase new-onset diabetes** (HR 0.95; 95% CI 0.83–1.09) and did not worsen HbA1c — a distinct advantage over statins.[21][18]
- **PCSK9 inhibitors:** In FOURIER, the diabetes subgroup (n = 11,031) had comparable RRR to those without diabetes. In ODYSSEY OUTCOMES, patients with diabetes had a **greater absolute risk reduction (2.3%)** than those with prediabetes (1.2%) or normoglycemia (1.2%). Neither PCSK9 mAb increased incident T2D or worsened glycemic control.[18][22]
- **Statins themselves** remain the foundation: a CTT meta-analysis of >18,000 patients with diabetes showed a **9% reduction in all-cause mortality and 13% reduction in vascular mortality per 1 mmol/L LDL-C reduction**, with benefit linearly related to LDL-C lowering.[18]

The ADA Standards of Care 2026 specifically recommend that in patients with diabetes who cannot tolerate statins, **ezetimibe and/or bempedoic acid** should be used, with PCSK9 mAbs for those not reaching goals.[18][15]

**5. Guideline Recommendations and Sequencing: Where They Agree and Differ**

**ACC/AHA 2026 Dyslipidemia Guideline** — the most recently updated guidance — provides the most detailed statin intolerance algorithm:[15]

1. Exclude secondary causes (hypothyroidism, drug interactions, vitamin D deficiency, excessive exercise)
2. Attempt rechallenge with a different statin or lower dose; consider alternate-day atorvastatin or rosuvastatin
3. For patients with **ASCVD**: add bempedoic acid, ezetimibe, or PCSK9 mAb (alone or combined) — Class I, LOE B-R. Notably, the 2026 guideline **no longer requires ezetimibe before PCSK9 mAb**; the choice may be based on degree of LDL-C lowering needed and patient preference[15]
4. For **high-risk primary prevention** (including diabetes with age criteria): bempedoic acid and/or ezetimibe first (Class I); PCSK9 mAb if goals not met (Class I)
5. Inclisiran is Class IIa for those unable to tolerate or obtain PCSK9 mAbs

**ESC/EAS 2019 Guideline** — uses a strict treat-to-target approach with lower LDL-C goals ([23][24][25]

**NICE (UK)** — recommends ezetimibe as first-line add-on or alternative to statins (TA385), with PCSK9 inhibitors (evolocumab TA394, alirocumab TA393) reserved for patients with established CVD and persistently high LDL-C despite maximally tolerated statin + ezetimibe. NICE has also approved bempedoic acid (TA694) for primary hypercholesterolemia in statin-intolerant patients. NICE maintains a **strict sequential approach**: statin optimization → ezetimibe → bempedoic acid → PCSK9 inhibitor, driven partly by cost-effectiveness thresholds.[26][24]

**French guidance:** The Société Française de Cardiologie (SFC) and Société Française d'Endocrinologie generally follow the ESC/EAS framework. The International Lipid Expert Panel (ILEP), which includes prominent French lipidologists (notably Bruckert), published a 2022 position paper recommending ezetimibe as the basis of initial non-statin therapy, with bempedoic acid and PCSK9 inhibitors added stepwise, and emphasizing the nocebo/drucebo effect as a major contributor to apparent intolerance.[24]

**2. Does the Recommendation Differ Between Sulfonylureas? Is Gliclazide Safer in CKD?**

**Yes — there is a pharmacokinetic rationale for considering gliclazide safer than glimepiride or glibenclamide in CKD**, though the clinical evidence is more nuanced than often stated.

**Pharmacokinetic basis:**

The key differentiator is metabolite activity. A comparative pharmacokinetic review shows that **gliclazide is metabolized hepatically to inactive metabolites**, with no change in drug exposure in renal impairment. In contrast, **glimepiride produces active metabolites** (M1 with ~33% of parent activity) that accumulate when GFR declines, and **glibenclamide produces active metabolites** that are 50% renally cleared with prolonged duration of action.[10][11]

**3. Evidence Base for the Thresholds: PK Data, Outcomes, or Consensus?**

The eGFR thresholds used in guidelines are based on **a combination of pharmacokinetic reasoning and expert consensus, not on prospective outcome trials** designed to identify a specific eGFR cut-off for sulfonylurea safety.

**Pharmacokinetic evidence:**

- The foundational data come from small PK studies (typically n = 10–30) showing accumulation of active metabolites of glimepiride and glibenclamide in patients with CrCl [10][11][12][4]

**Observational hypoglycemia data:**

- The CPRD cohort study (n = 120,803) provides the strongest epidemiological evidence that sulfonylurea-associated hypoglycemia risk increases sharply at **eGFR [13]

- The KDOQI 2012 guideline explicitly states that its SU recommendations are based on PK data and clinical experience, not on dedicated RCTs comparing SU safety across CKD stages. A separate, larger pooled analysis of **15 Sanofi phase 3 treat-to-target trials** (insulin-naïve T2D, glargine ± OADs, ≥24 weeks) stratified outcomes at thresholds of >0.5, >0.7, and >1.0 U/kg. Participants who exceeded each threshold had **higher mean HbA1c and FPG at endpoint, smaller HbA1c changes from baseline, greater weight gain, and higher likelihood of hypoglycemia** compared with those at or below the threshold.[1]

However, this evidence has been **criticized**. Davidson (2019) argued in a published response that the Umpierrez findings represent a "self-fulfilling prophecy": patients requiring higher U/kg doses are inherently more insulin-resistant, so the diminishing per-unit glycemic return reflects biology, not a true ceiling of basal insulin efficacy. Davidson noted that in the 15-trial pooled dataset, patients receiving >0.5 U/kg still achieved mean FPG of 120.7 mg/dL and HbA1c of 7.28% — only marginally worse than those at ≤0.5 U/kg (FPG 116.6 mg/dL, HbA1c 7.16%). He argued that **capping at 0.5 U/kg would unnecessarily push many patients to prandial insulin** when further basal titration could still achieve FPG targets.[2]

**Bottom line:** The 0.5 U/kg figure originated from post hoc dose-response analyses of treat-to-target RCTs and was subsequently adopted by AACE (first appearing in the 2023 algorithm) and referenced in ADA Standards of Care. It is best characterized as **pharmacodynamically informed expert consensus**, not a prospectively validated decision threshold.

---

**2. Randomized Trials Comparing Capping Basal Insulin vs. Continued Titration**

**No RCT has directly compared a strategy of capping basal insulin at 0.5 U/kg (or any threshold) and intensifying by another route versus continued unrestricted basal titration.** The closest relevant trial is:

- **DUAL V** (Lingvay et al., JAMA 2016): This open-label RCT (n = 557) compared **degludec/liraglutide fixed-ratio combination** (insulin capped at 50 U degludec) versus **continued up-titration of glargine U-100** (no dose cap) in patients with T2D uncontrolled on glargine 20–50 U + metformin. Degludec/liraglutide was **superior** for HbA1c reduction (−1.81% vs. −1.13%; estimated treatment difference −0.59%; P [3]
- The **2026 network meta-analysis** (Bertuol et al., Diabetologia, n = 19,122 across 58 RCTs) compared basal, basal-bolus, biphasic, and prandial insulin regimens. Basal-bolus achieved a modest additional HbA1c reduction of **−0.31%** (95% CI −0.45 to −0.17) over basal alone, but with ~1 kg greater weight gain and a borderline increase in severe hypoglycemia. No trial within this meta-analysis used a dose-based threshold as the trigger for intensification.[4]

The absence of a trial directly testing the 0.5 U/kg decision rule is a significant evidence gap acknowledged by multiple sources.[5][6]

**3. Other Markers of Overbasalization and Their Validation**

The **ADA Standards of Care 2026** identifies the following clinical signals of overbasalization:[7]

- **Bedtime-to-morning glucose differential ≥50 mg/dL (≥2.8 mmol/L)** — also known as the **BeAM value**
- **Hypoglycemia** (aware or unaware)
- **High glucose variability**
- **Elevated postprandial glucose (>180 mg/dL) with fasting glucose at goal**

The **AACE 2026 algorithm** uses two criteria: elevated PPG >180 mg/dL with FPG at goal, or basal dose >0.5 U/kg/day, and also references the BeAM score >50 mg/dL.[8][9]

**The BeAM value** is the best-studied alternative marker. It was developed as a simple SMBG-derived measure (bedtime glucose minus pre-breakfast glucose). A proof-of-concept study suggested that **BeAM values of 45–55 mg/dL (2.5–3.1 mmol/L) should trigger consideration of prandial intensification**. A post hoc analysis of the onset 3 trial (Peters et al., 2019) found that patients with baseline nocturnal glucose change >56 mg/dL (3.1 mmol/L) experienced **greater benefit from basal-bolus therapy** versus continued basal-only therapy in terms of HbA1c and PPG reduction, whereas those below this threshold gained little from bolus addition. The analysis also found that large BeAM values correlated with higher hypoglycemia incidence on basal-only therapy, suggesting that continued basal uptitration in these patients increases risk without proportional benefit.[10]

**Comparative validation:** No head-to-head study has compared the predictive performance of the BeAM value versus the 0.5 U/kg threshold for identifying patients who would benefit from intensification. The BeAM value has the advantage of being **physiologically grounded** (directly measuring the overnight glucose drop that reflects excessive basal insulin effect), whereas the weight-based threshold is a population-level pharmacodynamic observation. However, the BeAM evidence is also limited to **post hoc and exploratory analyses** from a single trial.[10]

The **American Family Physician 2026 review** synthesizes all three markers together, stating that overbasalization may be present when: (1) basal dose exceeds 0.5 U/kg without adequate control, (2) fasting glucose is frequently 50 mg/dL. It also notes that total daily doses approaching **>2 U/kg** should prompt evaluation for secondary causes of insulin resistance.[6]

**Summary**

The 0.5 U/kg/day threshold is **pharmacodynamically informed expert consensus**, not a prospectively validated decision rule. It derives primarily from the Umpierrez 2019 post hoc dose-response analysis and has been adopted by AACE (2023/2026) and referenced by ADA (2026). No RCT has tested capping basal insulin at this threshold versus continued titration. The BeAM value (≥50 mg/dL) is a complementary, physiologically grounded marker but is also supported only by exploratory post hoc data. In clinical practice, these markers should be used together — alongside assessment of PPG, hypoglycemia frequency, and glucose variability — rather than relying on any single threshold as an absolute decision rule.
